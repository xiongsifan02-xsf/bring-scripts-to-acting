Teaser

Figure 1. Motion generation and motion stylization either follow a caption or restyle an existing clip, whereas Script-to-Acting, given a script, a character, and a line, generates content-variant acting on a pretrained backbone. Emotion and control are enabled as a drop-in alignment signal, so both the strength and the motion identity of the performance change.

Method

Figure 2. Script-to-Acting. Given a script, a character, a line, and an intensity τ along the active emotion axis e⋆, Acting Mind produces an acting plan T. Acting Morph scores each script token against same-axis professional clips from TheaterAct, binding them into a token-wise acting-mode matrix S and encoding the selected columns as priors Href. Acting Creation then synthesizes motion conditioned on T and Href, yielding a content-variant family as τ is swept.

Acting across Emotion Axes

On the same character (Hamlet), each line is played along one of the six emotion axes A = {Anger, Disgust, Fear, Joy, Sadness, Neutral}. As the intensity is swept, the performance traverses distinct acting motifs rather than rescaling a single gesture.

Conclusion and Discussion

Conclusion. We presented Script-to-Acting, a unified framework for generating expressive, contextually aligned acting directly from scripts. Acting Mind, Acting Morph, and Acting Creation jointly enable continuous, content-variant control: changing emotion intensity can transform the action motif rather than merely rescaling a fixed motion. The modular design integrates with diverse motion-generation backbones and provides high-level, context-sensitive control over emotional and dramatic attributes. We further introduced TheaterAct, a multimodal dataset with rich annotations, establishing a foundation for script-conditioned acting generation.

Discussion. Our results suggest that emotion intensity in acting is better understood as a selection over a family of acting modes than as a global style gain: the same line can move across distinct motifs instead of only rescaling one motion. This points to a broader view of script-conditioned generation, in which the script is not merely a caption to be matched but a specification of dramatic intent that shapes which performance is produced. Our study further indicates that a small, carefully annotated theatrical corpus can expose behaviours that everyday-motion benchmarks leave invisible, which motivates domain-specific data as a driver of expressive generation.
